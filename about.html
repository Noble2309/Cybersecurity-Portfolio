How I approach security work

I prefer evidence-led assessment over noisy testing. That means understanding the exposed surface, establishing a baseline, validating the security boundary and preserving enough context that a finding can be reproduced, explained and remediated.

The same principle applies to client delivery. Technical accuracy matters, but so does communicating what the issue means, what should be prioritised and how remediation can be independently validated.

01Prove impact

Separate interesting behaviour from meaningful security consequences.

02Respect scope

Use minimum proof and stay within the agreed technical and business boundaries.

03Make remediation usable

Write findings so engineering teams know what to change and why.

Personal

A little more about me.

Technology, gaming and building things have been part of my life long before they became part of my career.

Technology has been part of my life for as long as I can remember. I grew up playing games across both computers and consoles, and over time that interest developed into wanting to understand the hardware and systems behind them rather than simply using them.

I have spent years building, upgrading and repairing my own computers, as well as helping friends and family with their systems. Whether that meant replacing components, diagnosing hardware or software problems, rebuilding machines or putting together complete PCs, I have always enjoyed working out how things function and solving problems when they do not.

That curiosity eventually became a much broader interest in computing, networking and cybersecurity, but technology is still something I enjoy outside of work rather than something I only interact with professionally.

I am also heavily interested in tabletop gaming, particularly Warhammer, including painting, playing and exploring the wider lore. Alongside that, I enjoy RPGs, strategy games, science fiction and fantasy, especially games and settings with strong world-building, interesting systems and plenty to learn.

More generally, I enjoy creating and building things simply for the experience of learning how they work, whether that is a computer, a technical lab, a small software project or a tabletop campaign. That curiosity and problem-solving mindset is a large part of what continues to draw me towards both technology and cybersecurity.
